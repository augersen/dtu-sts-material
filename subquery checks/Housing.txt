1. ID: 1951

   Actor: Dominic Machaelis
   Statement: Wind energy at sea is at its strongest, unaffected by land masses. The Energy Island has six large aerogenerators, three at a lower height (OMIT) to intercept the sea-hugging wind flow, while three are at greater heights to capture the upper wind flows. The hydraulic masts are able to adjust the heights of the aerogenerators to best harvest the wind potential from different directions.

Apart from its electricity contribution, the Energy Island is designed also to produce hydrogen. Because of the fluctuation of renewable energy resources, an energy storage system is required. The Energy Island relies on a hydrolysis plant to produce stored hydrogen and oxygen, part of which can be used by fuel cell technology as necessary to balance the power output for both external and internal uses. Part of the fuel can, in some cases, be tankered or pipelined to other destinations, when the electricity produced is not totally used by cable links to nearby destinations.

The Energy Island will also produce a base for Controlled Environment Agriculture (CEA) and Mariculture. In tropical latitudes, greenhouses need to be protected from the sun, a role performed by the heliostats, and well ventilated or evaporatively cooled. The 22-hectare platform can therefore be used for CEA, using part of the distilled water from the open OTEC cycle for hydroponics. If the floor is trellised, light can reach the seawater below, where pens would contain various forms of marine life.

Harbours and Housing: On the leeward side of the island or islands, the Energy Island needs to provide housing for staff, social facilities, workshops and harbours.

The Energy Island uses many diverse renewable energy sources, bringing their converted powers together in the most efficient way to provide electricity for the grid, and, by hydrolysis, hydrogen fuel for transport to nearby or distant population centers, where it can be used efficiently by fuel cell technology. The Energy Island can become a major supplier of electricity and hydrogen fuel for a pollution free society, and liberate the world from the perils of nuclear power generation, and from its political, economic and polluting dependence on dwindling fossil fuels. The Energy Island therefore constitutes a new departure, demonstrated to be all the more necessary by the visible signs of pollution and global warming, and the frightening fact that world energy use is expanding exponentially, having doubled in the last thirty years. Action is needed to bring about change and turn the tide.

Denmark has committed to a massive increase in
renewable energy production. For the North Seas
alone, a 300 GW (Giga Watt) wind power capacity
has been agreed for 2050. In comparison, the total
Danish offshore wind power capacity today is 2.3 GW.
Meeting these commitments through near-shore
wind power parks alone will just not be possible.
Even if existing local planning and protests from
citizens were disregarded, the available space
would fall short by a large margin. Thus, the
build-out needs to be based on the use of the
open sea wind resource.
Fortunately, the potential for open sea wind power
production is huge. However, the technical setup
around near-shore wind power parks cannot be
transferred to the open sea. At today’s near-shore
parks, power is produced as an alternating current
(AC) and transmitted directly to shore. This would
not work for an open sea park. Due to the longer
distance, the power must be converted into a
direct current (DC). The space requirements for the
converters and related equipment demand housing
at a physical structure close to the production site.
In other words, a build-out of the scale in question
would be impossible without energy islands.

Certain areas on land, including residences, housing areas, holiday home areas, and recreational spaces, are sensitive to changes in external noise. Such noise can have potential health effects, thereby affecting human well-being.

Calculations of airborne noise show that the Plan for Program Energiø Bornholm, involving 15 MW or 27 MW wind turbines, will not independently lead to exceeding noise limit values for total wind turbine noise in open country areas or areas designated for noise-sensitive land use. 

However, because existing land-based wind turbines at Sose already utilize the noise quota, the additional contribution from the offshore wind turbines will result in exceeding the noise limit for wind turbines. 

Therefore, the realization of the Plan for Program Energiø Bornholm, in conjunction with the existing wind turbines at Sose, can cause a high degree of impact and a significant effect on the population and human health, as the exceedance of a noise limit may increase. 

This potential significant impact could, however, be mitigated, for example, by modifying or dismantling the older wind turbines at Sose.

24. ID: 2163
   Year: 2024
   Cluster: (None)
   Actor: Lars Aagaard
   Statement: I have obtained contributions from the Danish Energy Agency to answer the question. The Danish Energy Agency informs: “Energinet, as a state-owned company, is responsible for the overall infrastructure in the electricity and gas sectors, according to the Energinet Act. Therefore, Energinet has planned for the connection of Energy Island Bornholm to the Zealand electricity grid. In Energinet’s planning, four possible connection points were assessed based on objective evaluation criteria, namely three existing 400 kV stations – Hovegård, Avedøre, and Bjæverskov – and a new station west of Copenhagen in the 400 kV grid at the branching point towards Copenhagen, later referred to as Station Solhøj. Energinet states that the following criteria were used for the overall assessment:
• Identification of derived grid limitations and the need for internal grid reinforcements
• The technical design of the project and the economic consequences of connecting the cable to Bornholm
• Considerations regarding the size of the potential land area, landscape conditions, extent of other buildings, need for purchasing/demolition of homes
• Technical consequences for the electricity system in terms of stability and robustness
• Possibility of co-location, e.g., placement of future PtX facilities
• Perspectives for the long-term development of the energy system
The assessment was based on the applicable analysis assumptions, specifically AF20. Based on an overall assessment of the four options, Energinet chose a new station, Solhøj, in 2021. Expansion of the existing stations at Avedøre and Hovegård was considered but rejected, according to the evaluation criteria above. Hovegård was rejected as it was assessed that it would not provide the same flexibility as Solhøj in connection with potential future grid expansions. Furthermore, connecting at Hovegård would require an expansion of the station in an area with more buildings and infrastructure than the existing station. Hovegård is geographically located somewhat less favorably in relation to integration with district heating and other infrastructure. Avedøre was rejected as it was assessed that Avedøre Power Plant as a connection point for Energy Island Bornholm is the most risky. Depending on whether associated electricity consumption is established alongside the connection of Energy Island, this would require one or two 400 kV reinforcements. Additionally, it is assessed that connecting Energy Island at Avedøre Power Plant could cause stability issues.” The former Minister approved Energinet’s recommendation in October 2022 regarding the Energy Bornholm eInfrastructure project, including the establishment of Station Solhøj, which is necessary for the grid connection of Energy Island Bornholm. The Minister also informed the KEF committee of the approval at the same time. The former Minister for Climate, Energy, and Utilities also requested in September 2022 that the then Minister for the Interior and Housing provide planning grounds for the grid connection of Energy Island Bornholm. As a result, a process has been initiated to prepare a national planning directive.

Another unresolved issue is housing the temporary workforce, expected to exceed 1,000 workers, during the installation phases. These workers typically stay in hotels and summerhouses during off-peak seasons. However, local hospitality services oppose this solution, as it could displace regular tourists and hurt long-term tourism revenue. Alternative, costlier accommodation solutions are needed, but local authorities have yet to address this challenge effectively.
