I hope politicians, civil servants, and project developers read the book. There is much inspiration to be drawn from it.

Tjele Langsø could be equipped with a giant hose connected to a large heat pump, which could provide free heat to the entire surrounding area. Before that, the phosphorus- and nitrogen-rich sludge covering the lakebed—currently suffocating the lake—could be removed and sent as free fertilizer to the Horn of Africa to help combat famine there. The geese that Ulrik Wilbek is eager to see disappear could, in their deceased state, be used in biogas plants, and once the geese are gone, perhaps swans and eagles killed by wind turbine blades could replace them.

The entire population of trash fish in the lake should be fished out to improve water quality, and these fish could also be used in biogas production. This would provide employment for fishermen who have lost their livelihoods due to the oxygen depletion in Danish waters and the consequent collapse of fish stocks. And so, we could continue.

The only requirement, as with Engineer Sidenius, is to disregard trivial banalities such as economic feasibility and environmental considerations. Then everything will run smoothly.

Apart from wind turbines, biogas, and solar panels, all the other elements of the so-called energy island are still undeveloped. Nonetheless, the economics of the project are claimed to be completely under control. There might be a few hundred million kroner for the citizens of Viborg and nearly free heating, and hopefully, a little will also go to Lykke-Per. Now, that’s quite certain.

Historically, in 2012, a favorable scheme was introduced, allowing private individuals to install solar panels on their homes and produce more electricity than they consumed. The surplus electricity could be deposited into the grid and retrieved when the solar panels were not producing enough.

The scheme was politically guaranteed to last at least 20 years, and solar panels were installed everywhere. They were installed where electricity was primarily consumed, which was entirely logical. Several municipalities sensibly wanted to install solar panels on their public buildings, but such anarchy could not be tolerated, and the state began losing revenue from energy taxes.

Municipalities were then required to create a separate company for each installation, which was obviously unmanageable. With the stroke of a pen, the scheme was rendered impossible. It was simply too favorable for private individuals.

Solar panels were installed everywhere, and citizens felt engaged in the green transition. But this could not be allowed to continue, so the scheme was quickly tightened and made less favorable. People stopped installing solar panels on a large scale. All that remained was the 20-year guarantee given to the first naïve individuals who believed in the state and political promises.

The then Minister for Energy, Supply, and Climate, Lars Chr. Lilleholt of the Liberal Party, was tasked with addressing this and solved it perfectly. The guarantee was abolished, effective January 1, 2020. That was the end of that. Let that be a lesson. Never trust a used car dealer or a politician.

Now solar panel owners receive about half the price for their surplus production compared to what they pay for electricity from their supplier. At times, like in the spring of 2021, it even cost private solar panel owners money to deliver electricity to the grid. You simply cannot have production where consumption also occurs—it’s pure anarchy.

What do we do, then? Since we cannot have self-sufficient electricity production in cities or factories where the electricity is immediately consumed, we must move to the countryside. This can solve several problems. A new scheme was introduced, allowing large-scale solar panel installations on agricultural land.

A skilled farmer can earn 6,000–7,000 kroner per hectare through food production. Remember—never trust a used car dealer or a politician, but this certainly does not apply to Liberal Party politicians when large agricultural interests need to be served.

It had to be done, and done effectively. A 20–30% advantage over traditional agricultural production wasn’t enough, so the scheme was designed so that by installing solar panels on good agricultural land, the landowner earns a net return of 15,000 kroner per hectare per year, without doing anything.

Now that’s something that works. Naturally, enterprising farmers are now lining up to install solar panels on their fields. You can’t blame them; it’s a win-win situation because:

Enterprising farmers are freed from the hassle of producing food and instead receive generous rewards. That’s good Liberal Party policy.
Much valuable farmland is removed from production. Reportedly, Denmark, after Bangladesh, is the most cultivated country in the world. This quickly rectifies that.
Nitrogen emissions into surrounding waters are significantly reduced.
Finally, electricity is produced in most places where it cannot be consumed locally, but an Engineer Sidenius solution can be found for that later. Always remember—ignore trivial banalities such as economic feasibility and environmental considerations. Problems are meant to be solved.
Attentive readers may already have noticed that Danish wind turbines often stand idle under favorable wind conditions. This happens because Danish wind turbines are already shut down up to 10% of the time due to what the wind industry calls "special regulation."

It’s simply impossible to utilize the electricity produced. However, here, too, a brilliant solution has been found. The Germans control and shut down Danish wind turbines—it’s not allowed in Germany—and simultaneously pay Danish wind turbine owners full compensation for lost production. That’s genius. A true Engineer Sidenius solution.

And so, everyone in the area became happy—the landowners, the mayor, and perhaps even Lykke-Per a bit more—but that was certainly well-deserved.
