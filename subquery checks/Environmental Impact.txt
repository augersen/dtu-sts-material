   Statement: Our position is that the siting of wind turbines is an integral component of Danish land-use planning. In 1994, all municipalities were tasked with designating sites for wind turbine groups and parks. By 1999, the responsibility for revising these plans was transferred to counties as part of their regional planning processes.  

This system of designated sites streamlined the installation of wind turbines, significantly facilitating development for wind power developers during the 1990s. However, as most of these designated sites were utilized during that period, the installation of new wind turbines now often requires the designation of additional sites. This process involves a more extended planning procedure, including conducting an Environmental Impact Assessment, which adds complexity to wind energy development.

2. ID: 6906
   Year: 2017
   Cluster: (None)
   Actor: Researchers
   Statement: We believe that transitioning to renewable energy sources can significantly reduce the environmental impact caused by traditional electricity generation methods. In the Graciosa project, we integrated a large-scale battery energy storage system with wind power and photovoltaics in a hybrid power plant. This system aims to replace the island's previous reliance on diesel combustion for electricity generation. Our Life Cycle Assessment results indicate that the environmental impacts have been reduced by approximately 43% due to the shift to this hybrid electricity supply, which incorporates 65% renewable energy.

Despite this progress, more than 60% of the environmental impacts are still attributed to the diesel-based electricity generation that remains in the system. The construction of the large-scale battery energy storage system accounts for only 6% of the total environmental impacts, while wind power and photovoltaics contribute 18% and 14%, respectively. This shows that while the renewable energy components help reduce impacts, there is still a significant reliance on diesel that needs to be addressed for further environmental improvement.

   Statement: Ørsted questions the value and feasibility of a caisson-based artificial island concept for the Danish “Energy Island” projects due to significant uncertainties in cost-effectiveness, construction risks, and operational efficiency. Specifically:

1. Economic Viability: The large-scale storage of hydrogen and Power-to-X technologies are still in their infancy, with significant uncertainty about whether offshore electrolysis can become competitive with onshore alternatives.

2. Construction Risks: Building a caisson-based artificial island involves high cost and timeline uncertainties due to sensitivity to wind and wave conditions, which could lead to significant delays. Additionally, the high environmental impact of such a project introduces further risks related to obtaining environmental approvals.

3. Operations and Maintenance (O&M): While Esbjerg is a highly competitive installation harbor, the willingness to pay for an island-based O&M facility is minimal. Furthermore, the large distances to wind turbines and challenging wave and wind conditions limit the use of smaller service vessels, making the island less practical as an operational base.

Ørsted advocates for alternative solutions that minimize these risks and uncertainties while ensuring cost-efficiency and environmental sustainability.

The energy cluster is central to this symbiosis, with facilities located close together to ensure short connections for efficient processes and to concentrate environmental impacts away from nearby neighbors.  

The energy cluster is expected to operate year-round, 24/7, although some components may pause during nighttime or for balancing or operational reasons. Electricity for the energy cluster will be sourced from the project's solar panels and nearby wind turbines. These sources will be directly connected to the cluster via a dedicated transformer station. The station will also connect to Denmark's electricity grid, enabling energy exchange when beneficial. However, the energy island is designed to be net self-sufficient in electricity, including power from the local wind turbines, which are owned and operated by another developer.
